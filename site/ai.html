What AI does here, and why it changes the odds for a non-technical person

You do not need to be a computer person to use this. You also do not need to be a finance person. You need a helper that is patient, never tired, and never tempted to gamble. That is what the AI is for.

One honest sentence first: the AI cannot tell you where prices will go next. Nobody's AI can. If anyone sells you one that claims to, walk away.

Claim, stated precisely: an AI orchestrator (Claude Code or a similar assistant that can read your journal, run scripts, enforce rules, build tools, and explain any term) changes the odds of behaving consistently, measuring honestly and learning faster. It does not change the odds that a given market goes up or down. The base rates in the reality check (about 1 in 14 challenge buyers ever paid) still apply; what this changes is the failure modes that sit behind them.

Bottom line: AI never predicts price, never places a trade for you, and never tells you to place one.

The coach who never sleeps

Think of it as a coach who never sleeps, never gets excited, and never trades. A coach who has no money on the table and cannot be talked into "just one more". It does four jobs.

1. Teacher

Ask it what any word means. Ask it again in simpler words. Ask it to quiz you. It will not make you feel silly. Use it with the glossary on the Learning page.

2. Bookkeeper

You write down each trade. It reads the list and does the maths: how often you win, what you make on average, where your losses cluster. You never need to learn spreadsheets.

3. Referee

Before a trade, it checks your own rules and the firm's limits. "Your stop is missing." "This trade is too big." "You are close to the daily loss line." It can say no. You cannot argue with a checklist at 2am, and that is the point.

4. Builder

It writes the small tools for you: the trade-size calculator, the journal statistics, the rule checker. You do not need to code. You ask, you check that it works, you use it.

The job it does NOT do

The one everyone hopes for: it does not know where the price goes next. It cannot pass the test for you either. Robots that try to do that get accounts closed.

So why does it matter?

The person in the video lost about 15 test accounts by breaking his own rules, often after a bad day. He had no referee. He had no bookkeeper that showed him the pattern. He only turned around when a mentor and public accountability gave him a coach. An AI helper can play much of that role at any hour, for the price of a subscription you may already have.

It will not make you a good trader. It makes it harder to cheat yourself, and faster to find out, cheaply, whether you can do this at all.

Why an orchestrator rather than a chatbot window: an assistant with file access reads the actual CSV rather than your summary of it, runs the scripts, and re-checks its own arithmetic. That removes transcription errors and "it sounded right" answers, which matter when the work is bookkeeping and rule checks.

Does AI predict prices? What the evidence says

Researchers have tried. Some chatbot-based systems looked good on old data. None has been shown to keep working in real life, once trading costs are paid. A system that wins on old data may just be fitted to the past, like learning last year's exam answers.

Even if one worked for a while, other people could copy it, and its edge would fade. That is why we use AI for the boring, useful jobs, and not as a crystal ball.

From research/tooling-and-learning.md section 5. Realistic, used today: journal review and weekly summaries from CSV; rule-enforcement scripts/EAs that hard-stop at loss thresholds; code generation for Pine alerts, pandas and backtest scaffolds (verify for look-ahead bugs); news-calendar summarisation; screeners on D1/H4 for setups you already defined. Hype: LLMs predicting price (near-martingale at retail horizons; any signal is stale and non-exclusive) and "AI prop-firm passing bots" (they trip hyperactivity, consistency and herd rules).

Evidence. Lopez-Lira & Tang (arXiv 2304.07619) found that ChatGPT headline sentiment correlates with next-day equity returns, in-sample. An LSE sentiment paper reports a Sharpe of 3.05 after costs on US equity news, which does not transfer to FX/gold technicals. TradingAgents (arXiv 2412.20138), a multi-agent LLM framework, beats baselines in backtest, not live. FinGPT is good at sentiment, weak at reasoning, and showed negative returns in the FinMem comparison. Ntale 2026 (arXiv 2607.15414) cites "numerical hallucination, context-window limits, inconsistent sideways-regime performance." Net: nothing credible shows LLMs reading charts into a durable FX/gold edge after costs.

Rules that matter if tempted (FTMO forbidden practices): EAs are allowed, but more than 2,000 server requests a day is "hyperactive"; no "AI/ultra-high-speed tools" for unfair advantage; no third party may trade your account; the same purchased EA across many accounts is flagged as herd trading; copying external masters is a termination offence.

How to use an AI assistant each week

Three small habits. Together they take about 10 minutes on Monday and 2 minutes on the days you trade.

  1. Monday, 10 min Press Export (top of the page). Open your AI assistant. Paste last week's journal rows and last week's review. Then paste the review prompt from the box below, exactly as it is. Read what it says. Pick one thing to change next week.
  2. Daily, 2 min Before any trade, tell your assistant your answers to the pre-trade checklist (see the Learning page). Ask it to say which answers are "no" or unclear. If any are, you do not trade.
  3. Monthly Ask the assistant to compare the firm's current rules page with the rules saved in your project. Firms change rules. A rule you did not know about is a rule you will break.

Monthly Ask it to re-verify the firm's rule page against tools/challenge_rules.json and list any differences (daily loss basis, trailing vs static drawdown, news window, consistency, weekend holding). The review prompt (copy it exactly)

Paste the last 7-30 days of journal/TRADE-LOG.csv rows and the previous week's review, then this:

You are reviewing a swing trader's journal. Do not comment on market direction or suggest trades. Using only the rows provided: (1) compute rule-adherence %, expectancy in R, win rate, net R, and max drawdown in R; (2) compare expectancy on rules_followed=Y vs N; (3) find clusters in losses by market, day of week, hour of trigger, or days held; (4) list every row where a checklist field is N and the trade was taken anyway; (5) compare this week with the previous review's "one thing I'll do differently" and say whether it happened; (6) draft the "one thing" for next week as a process change only. Be blunt. Numbers first, then at most 5 sentences of commentary.

Rules for AI in this project, and red flags

The four rules

  1. The AI never places a trade and never tells you to. It may say, "Rules 1 to 3 are met; rule 4 is not."
  2. Check its code. Any tool it writes for testing must be checked so it does not peek at the future. Ask it to show you how it handles dates and times.
  3. Its reviews describe the past. "Your losses cluster on Fridays." Changing a rule still needs a written decision.
  4. No AI tip services, no bought trading robots, no copying others. Firms close accounts for it, and the evidence says it does not work.

Red flags: walk away

  • Anyone selling "AI signals" or "AI picks".
  • A robot that "passes the test for you".
  • A trading robot (an "EA") you pay for, especially one thousands of others also use.
  • Anyone who offers to trade your account for you.
  • A group that copies someone else's trades.
  • Any "gem" that promises to double or triple in a day.

Firms can close your account and keep the fee for these.

Rules for using AI (docs/07)

  • An AI never places a trade and never tells you to place one. It can say "this chart meets conditions 1-3; condition 4 is not met."
  • Every AI-generated script that touches backtests is checked for look-ahead bias (using data from after the decision point). Ask it to show the timestamp logic.
  • AI review of the journal is descriptive ("your losses cluster on Fridays"); any rule change still goes through a decision record.
  • No "AI signal" products, no EA purchases, no copy trading. Firms terminate for it and the evidence says it doesn't work.

Red-flag list

  • AI signal sellers.
  • "AI prop-firm passing bots": they trip hyperactivity (over 2,000 server requests/day on FTMO), consistency and herd-detection rules.
  • Purchased EAs, especially the same one across many accounts (herd trading flag).
  • Third parties trading your account (forbidden).
  • Copying external masters (termination offence).
  • Tools that help evade copy/herd detection breach firm terms of service; do not build or buy them.
  • "Gems" promising 2-3x a day (docs/06, greed row).

Teaching others, and the product question

The order matters

  1. You first. Pass the practice-account stage. Until then nothing is proven.
  2. Then your first real payout. One real data point.
  3. Then your brother and wife. They start from the beginning on this site, with you as their mentor. Their fees come from their own budget, with the same cap.
  4. Only then think about a product, and only if two of the three of you pass the same practice stage.

Why so strict: teaching something unproven to family is how the course-selling cycle starts. If we teach, we teach the part that worked: accountability and a rule checker.
